What to do about it

Re-rated every morning; anything six months past its start date drops off, assumed done. Red do now  Amber do soon  Green on the horizon.

  • Red · do now

    Data-protection complaints procedure

    Source ↗

    What it is: You must acknowledge data-protection complaints within 30 days, in force since 19 June 2026.

    Why it matters: Missing the 30 days is a breach in itself, whatever happens with the complaint.

    Do now: Confirm in writing who receives data complaints and that they know to acknowledge in writing within 30 days.

    Then later: Re-confirm whenever you update your privacy notice or data-protection policy.

  • Red · do now

    Harassment duty & wider discrimination risk

    Source ↗

    What it is: The duty to take "all reasonable steps" against sexual harassment, plus August rulings on belief, health, maternity and misconduct dismissals.

    Why it matters: Since 30 October you're liable for harassment by customers, clients and contractors unless you've taken those steps.

    Do now: Brief managers: act on how a belief is expressed, not the belief; get medical input before assuming misconduct; document any job decision during leave; evidence every dismissal; abusive customers are your problem too.

    Then later: Check your harassment risk assessment against the EHRC's updated eight-step guide.

  • Red · do now

    Audit flat fees & benefits for part-time impact

    Source ↗

    What it is: Part-time status need only be an effective cause of worse treatment, not the sole cause (Supreme Court, 12 Aug).

    Why it matters: Flat fees, deductions or benefit rules that cost part-timers more are now far easier to challenge.

    Do now: List every fixed fee, deduction or minimum-hours benefit rule and flag any that land harder on part-time staff.

    Then later: Pro-rate or objectively justify flagged rules before your next pay or benefits review.

  • Red · do now

    Right-to-work checks now cover contractors & gig staff

    Source ↗

    What it is: Since 1 October, checks also cover workers, contractors and gig staff, not just employees.

    Why it matters: Penalties: £45k per worker, £60k for repeats; a labour supplier's failures can pass to you. Existing staff engaged before 1 October need no retrospective check.

    Do now: List non-employees not yet checked, run the checks and log who and when. If you use a digital ID provider, confirm it's on the OfDIA register for right-to-work.

    Then later: Document a substitute-identity check and build checks into onboarding.

  • Red · do now

    Union access-request process & equality-rep rights

    Source ↗

    What it is: Recognised unions can request access to workplaces with 21+ staff; trained equality reps get reasonable paid time off and facilities.

    Why it matters: You have 15 working days to respond; penalties run from £75k for a first breach to £500k for repeats, and refused equality reps can claim.

    Do now: Confirm who receives and logs access requests, check you can meet the 15-day clock, and tell managers what equality reps can ask for.

    Then later: Log every request and review the process after the first few.

  • Amber · do soon

    The January cliff: six-month unfair dismissal, then fire-and-rehire in March

    Source ↗

    What it is: Six-month unfair dismissal with no compensation cap starts 1 January 2027; the fire-and-rehire ban follows in March 2027.

    Why it matters: Staff you can dismiss at low risk today gain full rights after six months; dismissing to force pay, hours or shift changes becomes automatically unfair.

    Do now: Set documented probation reviews inside the first six months, and list any planned changes to pay, hours or shifts to check against the ban.

    Then later: Train managers to document performance concerns from day one.

  • Amber · do soon

    Holiday pay audit before Fair Work Agency enforcement

    Source ↗

    What it is: The Fair Work Agency takes on holiday-pay and full minimum-wage enforcement from April 2027.

    Why it matters: Poor records are a criminal offence (unlimited fine); wrong holiday pay risks a penalty of 200% of the underpayment (capped at £20,000 per worker).

    Do now: Sample one pay run: check the log shows hours, holiday accrued and how it was calculated, and lowest-paid staff clear April 2026 rates after deductions.

    Then later: Complete a full audit before enforcement starts.

  • Amber · do soon

    Evidence market-forces pay gaps before a claim lands

    Source ↗

    What it is: Genuine recruitment and retention pressures can justify paying one equal-value role more (Next v Thandi, EAT, 7 Sep).

    Why it matters: Without evidence you're exposed to a claim: "that's just the rate" isn't enough, and premium pay isn't covered.

    Do now: List equal-value roles paid differently and note the evidence behind each gap: vacancy-fill times, local pay benchmarks, turnover.

    Then later: Add this to your next pay review and watch for a Court of Appeal outcome.

    Case law watch

    Recent decisions

    • Next Retail & Next Distribution v Thandi

      [2026] EAT 130, 7 Sep. Market pressures justified higher warehouse basic pay, but most of the finding for about 3,500 claimants stands.

      What it could change: Only partly: a usable defence on basic pay if you have evidence, not on premium pay, and the claimants plan to appeal.

    • McManus v British Airways

      [2026] EAT 155. Appeal dismissed, but known mental ill-health is relevant to whether a remark had a harassing effect.

      What it could change: Nothing new in principle: check what you knew about someone's mental health before dismissing a complaint about a remark.

    • Augustine v Data Cars

      [2026] UKSC 30, 12 Aug. Part-time status need only be an effective cause of worse treatment, not the sole cause.

      What it could change: New binding precedent: claims over flat fees and benefit rules are easier to bring, though objective justification remains a defence.

    • Fieldfisher v Rustambekov

      [2026] EAT 99. The tribunal wrongly replaced the firm's reasonable conclusions with its own; sent back for a rehearing.

      What it could change: Nothing new: it confirms the band of reasonable responses test, and whether this dismissal was fair goes back to a fresh tribunal.

    • Wolfe v Taka Mayfair

      [2026] EAT 106, 22 Jul. The right to be accompanied only arises once the worker asks for a companion.

      What it could change: Nothing yet: it confirms the 1999 Act, but failing to explain a meeting's purpose can still count against you on unfair dismissal.

    • University of Bristol v Miller

      [2026] EAT 84. Anti-Zionist belief is protected and the dismissal was discriminatory; eight of the university's nine grounds failed.

      What it could change: Liability is settled at appeal level, so controversy alone doesn't justify dismissal, but the compensation is still open.

    • Geeks v Watts

      [2026] EWCA Civ 889, 10 Jul. An £8,108 training clawback was an unenforceable restraint of trade.

      What it could change: New binding precedent: courts look at a clawback's real effect on leaving, though tightly drawn clauses tied to real costs can still hold.

    • Peggie v NHS Fife & Dr Upton

      EAT permission granted 30 Jul on all 13 grounds; full hearing expected early 2027.

      What it could change: Nothing yet: keep any changing-room policy easy to revise, because the December 2025 ruling is still contested.
