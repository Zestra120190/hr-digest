Priority actions

What to do about it

Every live obligation or upcoming change, rated and re-checked daily. Red do now  Amber do soon  Green on the horizon.

  • Red · do now

    Six-year holiday pay records

    What it is: Employers must keep a record of every worker's hours, pay and holiday-accrual calculation going back six years.

    Why it matters: The Fair Work Agency's holiday pay look-back has run since 18 December 2025 — gaps in your records now can't be recreated once a claim lands.

    Do now: Set up (or check) a dated log per worker showing hours worked, holiday accrued and how each holiday payment was calculated, updated every pay run.

    Then later: Full internal audit before the Fair Work Agency starts actively enforcing holiday pay from 2027.

  • Red · do now

    Day-one paternity & parental leave wording

    What it is: Paternity leave and unpaid parental leave became day-one rights — no minimum service required — from 6 April 2026.

    Why it matters: A handbook that still shows a qualifying period is giving staff incorrect information and could support an unfair-treatment complaint.

    Do now: Open your paternity and parental leave policy today and delete any reference to a minimum length of service.

    Then later: Roll the corrected wording into the next handbook reissue and brief managers who approve leave requests.

  • Red · do now

    Data-protection complaints procedure

    What it is: A duty to acknowledge data-subject complaints (e.g. queries about a subject access request) within 30 days, in force since 19 June 2026.

    Why it matters: Missing the 30-day acknowledgement is itself a breach, whatever happens with the underlying complaint.

    Do now: Confirm in writing who in your business receives a data complaint and that they know to send a written acknowledgement within 30 days.

    Then later: Re-confirm the process each time you update your privacy notice or data-protection policy.

  • Red · do now

    Harassment duty & wider discrimination risk

    What it is: The standing duty to take "all reasonable steps" to prevent harassment, plus this month's tribunal and EAT rulings on belief, health and maternity decisions.

    Why it matters: Employers have just been penalised — tens of thousands each — for treating a protected belief's manifestation as automatic misconduct, assuming intoxication over a health condition, and letting a role lapse during maternity leave.

    Do now: Brief managers on three rules: deal with how a belief is expressed through your normal conduct process, not the belief itself; get medical input before assuming a health explanation isn't behind an incident; and give any decision affecting someone's job during leave the same documented justification as any other dismissal.

    Then later: Fold these into your next harassment risk assessment and manager training refresh; third-party harassment liability adds from 30 October.

  • Amber · do soon

    Extend right-to-work checks

    What it is: Right-to-work checks extend to cover workers, contractors and gig-economy staff, not just employees.

    Why it matters: From 1 October 2026 the draft Code confirms penalties of £45k per worker for a first breach (£60k for repeats), plus a new duty to check substitute workers' identities.

    Do now: List every non-employee currently engaged (contractor, agency worker, gig staff) and flag who hasn't had a right-to-work check yet.

    Then later: Have checks completed and a substitute-identity process documented before 1 October.

  • Amber · do soon

    Union access-request process

    What it is: A formal right-of-access regime lets recognised unions request access to workplaces with 21+ employees.

    Why it matters: From 30 October 2026 employers must respond to a request within 15 working days — breaching agreed terms risks penalties up to £500k.

    Do now: Identify who would receive and log a union access request, and draft a simple response process built around the 15-day clock.

    Then later: Test that whoever covers HR knows the process before the regime goes live on 30 October.

  • Amber · do soon

    Tighten probation & performance management

    What it is: The unfair dismissal qualifying period falls from two years to six months, with the compensation cap removed.

    Why it matters: From 1 January 2027, staff you can currently dismiss with low risk in their first two years gain full unfair dismissal rights after just six months.

    Do now: Check your probation policy sets clear, documented review points inside the first six months, not just at month 11.

    Then later: Train managers to document performance concerns from day one, ready for the January 2027 change.

  • Green · on the horizon

    Fire-and-rehire ban & legacy agreements

    What it is: Fire-and-rehire becomes automatically unfair from 1 January 2027; separately, this week's Bank of Baroda ruling confirms decades-old union collective agreements can still bind an employer today.

    Why it matters: No action is due yet, but any restructuring or redundancy plan made now should assume the tighter fire-and-rehire rules, and shouldn't rely on current severance terms without checking for a legacy agreement.

    Do now: Add a note to your restructuring/redundancy playbook to check for legacy collective agreements and to assume the incoming fire-and-rehire rules.

    Then later: Revisit when the ban lands 1 January 2027, or before any redundancy exercise.

This week & recent

What's moving right now

Developments from the last few weeks that change what HR should be doing.

  • 04 Aug 26

    Anti-Zionist belief confirmed protected as University of Bristol loses discrimination appeal

    The Employment Appeal Tribunal dismissed the University of Bristol's challenge to a 2024 ruling that Professor David Miller's anti-Zionist beliefs are a protected philosophical belief under the Equality Act 2010, and that dismissing him over comments manifesting that belief was direct discrimination and unfair dismissal (University of Bristol v Miller [2026] EAT 84).

    Do: a belief can be protected even if some find it offensive — deal with how it's expressed (conduct, harassment of colleagues) through your normal process, not by treating holding the belief itself as misconduct.
  • 04 Aug 26

    Bipolar-disorder worker wins £50k after employer wrongly assumed she was drunk

    A Cambridge tribunal ordered AT Transport Solutions to pay customer liaison manager Samantha Dallimore £49,612 after finding disability discrimination and unfair dismissal: she was suspended and sacked for being "under the influence" when slurred speech, drowsiness and confusion were in fact caused by her bipolar medication, and the tribunal found the firm should have sought medical help and got her home safely rather than assume intoxication.

    Do: when someone shows signs that could be drink, drugs or a health condition, get medical help and ask before assuming misconduct — treating a possible disability episode as intoxication is a discrimination risk in itself.
  • 04 Aug 26

    Mother sacked during year-long maternity leave awarded £51k

    A tribunal found a security equipment manufacturer unlawfully dismissed an employee while she was on maternity leave that had run for a year, awarding her £51,126 for pregnancy and maternity discrimination and unfair dismissal.

    Do: don't let a role quietly disappear while someone is on leave — any decision affecting a job during maternity leave needs the same objective, documented justification as any other dismissal, or it risks an automatic discrimination finding.
  • 03 Aug 26

    Sandie Peggie granted permission to appeal NHS Fife tribunal ruling

    The EAT confirmed Sandie Peggie can proceed to a full appeal against December 2025's partial ruling — which upheld her harassment complaint over sharing a changing room with a transgender doctor but dismissed her direct and indirect discrimination and victimisation claims — after Judge Tayler found reasonable grounds across 13 of her appeal points. NHS Fife has 28 days to respond; a full hearing is expected in early 2027.

    Do: treat first-instance findings on live, contested issues like single-sex facilities as provisional, not settled precedent — the discrimination findings in this case are going back to appeal, not just the harassment claim upheld against the employer.
  • 03 Aug 26

    Bank loses fight over a decades-old redundancy deal

    An employment tribunal ruled that Bank of Baroda wrongly denied four former employees the enhanced redundancy pay — four weeks' pay per year of service — promised under a collective agreement its recognised union struck around 30 years ago, rejecting the bank's argument that the old terms no longer applied.

    Do: check whether legacy collective agreements or long-standing union deals are still contractually binding before relying on current policy to set a redundancy payout — old terms don't lapse just because nobody's relied on them in years.

Legislation radar

Employment Rights Act 2025 — the rollout

Royal Assent 18 Dec 2025; provisions commence in waves. Green is in force, amber is next, grey is further out.

  • 18 FEB 2026 In force

    Industrial action reforms

    Strike notice cut 14 → 10 days, ballot mandates extended 6 → 12 months, new protection from dismissal for industrial action.

  • 6 APR 2026 In force

    The April package

    SSP from day one with the lower earnings limit removed · day-one paternity & unpaid parental leave · collective-redundancy protective award doubled to 180 days' pay · six-year holiday pay record-keeping duty · Fair Work Agency established · whistleblowing protections widened.

  • 25 AUG 2026 —

    Electronic balloting

    Final Code of Practice published 28 July confirms e-voting and hybrid voting go live for industrial action, political fund and union election/merger ballots — six days earlier than the 31 August originally trailed; workplace voting also permitted for industrial-action ballots with employer consent. Repeal of the 50% turnout threshold expected (timing tbc); recognition/derecognition ballots follow in 2027.

  • 1 OCT 2026 —

    The October package

    Tribunal time limits 3 → 6 months · workers must be told of their right to join a union · expanded right-to-work checks. Combined with the 12-week Acas Early Conciliation window (live since Dec 2025; government reviews whether it stays at 12 weeks in October 2026), some claims could reach nine months start-to-finish.

  • 30 OCT 2026 —

    Union access rights & harassment duty

    Access regime live for employers with 21+ employees; agreed-terms breaches attract penalties up to £500k. Also from this date (delayed from 1 Oct): the "all reasonable steps" duty to prevent sexual harassment, liability for third-party harassment, and a new right not to suffer detriment for taking industrial action — deducting strike-day pay stays lawful, but a detriment failure now joins the list for a 25% Acas uplift.

  • LATE 2026 Ahead

    Autumn stragglers

    Fair pay agreement process launches in adult social care; tipping reforms rescheduled from October to end of 2026; Mandatory Seafarers' Charter targeted for December (NMW-equivalent pay and rest for cross-Channel crew).

  • 1 JAN 2027 Ahead

    The January cliff

    Unfair dismissal qualifying period drops to six months and the compensatory cap goes · fire-and-rehire becomes automatically unfair (incl. varying pay, pensions, hours, shifts, holidays) · replacing an employee with a non-employee becomes automatically unfair · new "organisation-wide" collective-redundancy consultation threshold (pushed back from Oct 2026).

  • 2027 Ahead

    Through 2027

    Bereavement leave incl. pregnancy loss before 24 weeks · zero-hours guaranteed-hours contracts and shift notice · NDAs banned in harassment/discrimination cases · tighter flexible-working refusal test · mandatory gender equality action plans · FWA starts enforcing holiday pay.

  • 2028+ Ahead

    On the horizon

    Ethnicity and disability pay gap reporting — first snapshot unlikely before April 2028, reporting from 2029. Employment status review and TUPE reform still pending.

Case law watch

Recent decisions

  • University of Bristol v Miller

    Employment Appeal Tribunal [2026] EAT 84: dismissed the university's appeal against a 2024 finding that Professor David Miller's anti-Zionist beliefs are protected under the Equality Act 2010, and that dismissing him over comments manifesting that belief was direct discrimination and unfair. A belief can be protected even where its expression is contentious.

  • Dallimore v AT Transport Solutions Ltd

    Employment tribunal (Cambridge, 3306629/2024): disability discrimination and unfair dismissal upheld after a customer liaison manager with bipolar disorder was suspended and sacked for being "under the influence" — the panel found no explanation but her disability, and that the firm should have sought medical help. £49,612 awarded. Rule out a health explanation before assuming intoxication.

  • Bank of Baroda redundancy dispute

    Employment tribunal: the bank wrongly denied four former employees enhanced redundancy pay — four weeks per year of service — promised under a c.1994 collective agreement with its recognised union, rejecting its argument the terms had lapsed. Old collective agreements can still bind decades later.

  • Peggie v NHS Fife Health Board & Dr Beth Upton

    Employment Appeal Tribunal: permission granted for a full appeal on 13 grounds against December 2025's partial ruling (harassment upheld; discrimination and victimisation claims dismissed) over shared changing-room use with a transgender doctor. NHS Fife has 28 days to respond; hearing expected early 2027. Treat the underlying findings as still contested.

  • Harber v Intrinsic Care Group

    Employment tribunal: withdrawing a written job offer for a service manager role after the candidate disclosed a breast cancer diagnosis and recent surgery, without exploring adjustments, was direct disability discrimination. £16,732 awarded, including £10,000 for injury to feelings. A written offer already carries obligations.
